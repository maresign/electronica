The gap

A bill of lading issued digitally in Shanghai is worth nothing in Istanbul if the bank, the customs officer or the court cannot check it without asking the issuing platform.

Every eBL platform is a closed world: its own keys, its own registry, its own definition of "valid". Cargo that starts electronic abroad turns back into paper at the Turkish border — and when a platform's certificate expires, or the platform disappears, the evidence goes with it. DCSA's own reading: the brake is not technology, it is trust and legal recognition.

What we build

1

Read anything

TradeTrust / OpenAttestation, DCSA eBL envelopes, qualified e-signatures and seals — parsed without the issuing platform's software.

2

One policy engine

X.509 trust lists, platform key registries and issuer-domain proofs resolved in a single policy, producing an ETSI EN 319 102-1 equivalence report.

3

Evidence that survives

A sealed evidence package — hybrid ECDSA + ML-DSA-65 (FIPS 204) — that stays verifiable after certificates, platforms and algorithms expire.

Two faces, one engine. Outward: a neutral verification layer that sits under international eBL platforms, banks, insurers and customs. Inward: a sovereign Turkish leg for eBL, e-CMR and eFTI — so that digital cargo does not have to become paper again while national law catches up with MLETR.

Proof, not slideware

  • Working engine, running in your browser. Seven test cases — valid chains, tampered documents, deleted transfers, unknown platforms — all with real cryptography, no server round-trip.
  • Cross-verified. The same evidence packages validate in two independent implementations and against OpenSSL.
  • Honest about limits. Sample documents are synthetic; on-chain registry lookups and qualified timestamps land in the pilot phase.
